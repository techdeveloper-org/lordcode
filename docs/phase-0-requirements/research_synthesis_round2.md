## 3. Progressive Summary — Step 3: Section Distillation, Grading, and Gap Classification

### 3.1 Q1 — OpenAI OAuth third-party eligibility (updated)

**Finding A — core eligibility question.** Still unconfirmed. No OpenAI-owned source, including the newly-checked platform docs index, contains an explicit statement admitting or excluding third-party CLIs from "Sign in with ChatGPT."

**GRADE: VERY LOW** (unchanged from round 1). Reasoning: the protocol's own rule applies literally — no explicit on-domain PRIMARY statement was found, so the fallback grade holds. This round strengthens the *confidence that the gap is real and not a navigation artifact* (the docs index was checked and the topic is absent from the site's organizational structure, not just from one guessed URL), but strengthening confidence that an absence is genuine does not raise the grade of what is, definitionally, still an absence.

**Gap classification:** **Empirical gap**, and more confidently so than round 1. Round 1 left open whether the dead link (`platform.openai.com/docs/guides/authentication`) might be hiding relevant content reachable by a different path — a narrow **Reporting gap** nested inside the broader question. Round 2 closes that specific Reporting gap: `developers.openai.com/api/docs` is the current, working platform docs index, its full authentication-topics navigation was read, and no page on third-party/general "Sign in with ChatGPT" OAuth appears anywhere in it. The content is not hiding behind a different index entry; it is simply not organized under this docs tree. What remains is a clean Empirical gap — the evidence-map cell is empty, checked via the correct current path, not merely inaccessible.

**Finding B — new this round — Codex OAuth client architecture.** The shipped Codex CLI OAuth implementation (`codex-rs/login/src/server.rs`, `default_client.rs`) is bound to a fixed issuer (`auth.openai.com`), uses a redirect URI that a source comment states must be kept "in sync with the Codex CLI Hydra redirect URI allow-list," and stamps every request with a hardcoded originator (`codex_cli_rs`) plus an internal residency header.

**What this evidence DOES establish (GRADE: HIGH for this narrow claim):** the actual, shipped OAuth client used by Codex CLI is architecturally a gated, allow-listed, single-purpose client — not a generic, publicly self-registerable OAuth client the way a Google or GitHub OAuth app typically is. This is PRIMARY, first-hand source code, the strongest evidentiary tier available short of an explicit vendor policy statement, and it directly answers the narrow question "is Codex's OAuth client open-registration or gated" with a clear "gated."

**What this evidence does NOT establish (GRADE: VERY LOW for the broader claim):** it does not show that third parties are excluded from "Sign in with ChatGPT" as a general mechanism, that no other client IDs exist or could be registered, or that OpenAI has any stated policy on the matter. An allow-list architecture is consistent with several different underlying truths — "no one outside OpenAI may register," "a registration process exists but is undocumented publicly," or "registration exists but this pass did not find it" — and the source code cannot distinguish between them. Treating "Codex's specific client is gated" as equivalent to "OpenAI generally excludes third parties" would be exactly the indirectness problem GRADE's downgrade criteria warn about (source-evaluation-credibility-core §4.1): the evidence answers an adjacent question, not the one asked. It must be reported as "strong circumstantial PRIMARY evidence of a gated, Codex-specific implementation," per the round-2 findings document's own wording — not upgraded to "confirmed first-party-only policy."

**Single artifact that would close this:** `https://help.openai.com/en/articles/20001410-sign-in-with-chatgpt` — an OpenAI-owned Help Center article titled exactly "Sign in with ChatGPT," currently returning HTTP 403 and unreached in either round. This is the single most on-point named source across both rounds; if fetched and read, it would very likely resolve Q1 to HIGH in one direction or the other. `github.com/openai/codex/issues/10974` (404 on both the HTML and REST routes) is a secondary target of unknown value — its 404 status cannot be distinguished from "renumbered/transferred" versus "deleted/access-restricted," so it is a lower-priority retry than the Help Center article.

**Remaining unreached items — gap classification:**
- `help.openai.com/.../sign-in-with-chatgpt` (403): **Reporting gap.** An OpenAI-owned page with an on-point title is indexed and known to exist; it is currently unreadable due to an access-layer error, not confirmed to contain nothing relevant.
- `github.com/openai/codex/issues/10974` (404 both routes): **Reporting gap, weakly held.** The round-2 findings document itself states this correctly — "may be renumbered, transferred, or access-restricted; not confirmed either way." A 404 on a numbered GitHub issue is closer to ambiguous than a Reporting gap on a named, still-live page, but it cannot be classified as an Empirical gap (nothing to find) either, since the issue may simply have moved.

### 3.4 Q3 — Combined verdict across both rounds, corrected for the duplicate

**The round-2 findings document states: "all four candidates surfaced across both rounds now resolve to the same category."** This is imprecise and must be corrected here rather than passed through, per Rule #4 (contradictions and imprecisions are findings, not errors to smooth over) and per the explicit instruction to address the duplicate-candidate finding directly.

**Corrected count: three independent candidates, not four.**
1. `agency-agents` (msitarzewski) — community marketplace, MIT-licensed. *Also* the underlying content behind AgentsRoom's "270 expert agents" claim (same artifact, two storefronts).
2. `mcpservers.org/agent-skills` — multi-contributor community directory.
3. `claude-skills` (alirezarezvani) — single-maintainer, MIT-licensed, user-installed library.

AgentsRoom is not a fourth candidate; it is a distribution channel/rebrand of candidate 1. Counting it separately — which is what "four candidates... all resolve to the same category" implicitly does — is the precise anti-pattern research-analytics-bibliometrics-core's anti-pattern list names: treating a correlated/derivative source as if it contributed independent convergent weight (the same logic as source-evaluation-credibility-core §5.2's triangulation-independence caveat — "sources are rarely independent... common upstream source ≈ single source in information value"). AgentsRoom and `agency-agents` share the exact same upstream origin; they are one data point, not two.

**What this does to the strength of the Q3 conclusion, stated plainly:** the round-2 findings document's own framing overstates the breadth of confirmation by one-third (4 vs. the true 3). This cuts *against* LordCode's interest in the strict sense that it reduces, not inflates, the apparent evidentiary base for "no comparable vendor-bundled corpus was found" — fewer independent checks were actually performed than the raw candidate count implied, even though all three that were genuinely independent still resolve the same direction. The direction of the finding is unchanged; its breadth is smaller than as-reported, and that must be visible, not quietly dropped.

**GRADE: LOW** — an upgrade from round 1's VERY LOW, but still within the protocol's binding cap (§9: "never higher" than LOW). Reasoning for the upgrade within the cap: round 1's VERY LOW was explicitly reasoned as "saturation was met only by the weaker-looking candidates, while the two strongest-looking leads sat unexamined" — a Reporting-gap-driven penalty. Round 2 closes both of those Reporting gaps with PRIMARY-tier fetches; nothing is now sitting unexamined among the leads this research effort actually surfaced. Reasoning against going higher than LOW, beyond the hard protocol cap: (a) the true independent-candidate count is 3, not the 4 implied by the round-2 document's own framing — a materially thin base for a "landscape" claim of any strength; (b) this remains, structurally, a search-budget-limited discovery process (a total of 5 WebSearch calls and roughly 6 named candidate products across two dispatch rounds), not a systematic or exhaustive competitor inventory — an Empirical gap persists at the macro level (the full landscape of coding tools that might ship a bundled specialist corpus was never enumerated, only sampled); (c) per research-methodology-core, absence of evidence in a sampled, non-exhaustive search is not evidence of absence in the underlying population.

**Recommendation to the human on the cap itself (reported, not silently substituted for the operative grade):** Do not raise the LOW/VERY LOW ceiling for Q3 on the strength of this round's findings. The premise that might have justified reconsidering the cap — "four independent candidates all resolving the same way" — turned out, on inspection, to be three, and three data points from a budget-capped, non-exhaustive search is not a stronger basis for raising a deliberately conservative ceiling than what round 1 had. If anything, the duplicate-candidate discovery is a caution about trusting apparent-breadth-of-convergence numbers at face value before verifying independence, and argues for *keeping* the cap exactly where the protocol set it, not loosening it. **The operative grade for the PRD/README is LOW, not any higher, and the reasoning above is why — not a substitute for — that operative grade.**

**Gap classification, still valid at the macro level:** **Empirical gap** — the landscape itself (not any specific named candidate) remains unswept beyond the ~6 candidates checked across two rounds. This is distinct from a Reporting gap (a specific known-to-exist source that is inaccessible) — there is no known specific fourth-plus candidate sitting unresolved; rather, an unknown number of un-surfaced candidates may exist that neither round's search budget reached.
